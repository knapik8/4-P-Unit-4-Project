The Arab-Israeli War
Preceding these wars was the Arab-Israeli War of 1948. On the midnight of May 14th, Britain's Mandate for Palestine expired, the European nation finally pulling out of the land after some 28 years. The Mandate, along with others for different territories, was created after WWI by the League of Nations when distributing said territories from one nation to another. Britain received Mandatory Palestine after the fall of the Ottoman Empire; they’d defeated the Ottoman troops occupying the land in 1917-1918.

On this same night, David Ben-Gurion declared Israel's independence and formal existence as a new country. He would become Israel's first Prime Minister. Ben-Gurion had served as the main leader of the Jewish community in Mandatory Palestine leading up to its eventual independence.

An important document to note is the Balfour Declaration of 1917; a British declaration supporting the establishment of a Jewish homeland in Mandatory Palestine. Due to factors like the creation and rise in Zionism and escaping persecution in other areas, Jews were immigrating to Mandatory Palestine (or the Ottoman Empire pre-1920) en masse throughout the early-to-mid 20th century. This document was controversial among Arabs who felt that the land should be solely theirs. There were later British declarations, such as the 1939 White Paper, that retracted certain statements, limiting things like Jewish immigration to Mandatory Palestine, or limiting the amount of land Jews could buy there. They were proclaimed to placate the Arabs after myriad revolts.

Another document to note was the United Nations Partition Plan for Palestine created in 1947. It split the land bicamerally; one half for Arabs, the other for Jews. Jerusalem was an international point. While Jewish leaders accepted this plan, the Arab leaders did not. However, it was the solution decided upon for when the Mandate dissolved.



The first instance of an offered partition plan was in 1937 with the Peel Commission. This plan gave a vast majority of the land to the Arabs, with a northern part of the nation reserved for the Jews, as well as a small British enclave. The plan was rejected by the Arabs, however the Jews, though upset with what little land they were to be given, accepted the plan given that there were further negotiations to be made. The plan wasn’t ever instated due to the intensity of Arab refusal. Throughout history, peace plans have been officially offered four times: in 1937, 1947, 2000, and 2008. All four times, they’d been rejected by Arab leaders. ‘Palestine’ has never once existed as a sovereign nation. But I digress; this opens a whole other can of worms, and the focus of this site is on the 20th century.

So, on May 15th of 1948, a coalition of Arab nations, that being Egypt, Jordan, and Syria, attempted to attack the newly founded nation of Israel. They invaded ‘Palestine’ (the orange areas on the map above), took control, and started attacking Israeli settlements/forces. Something else to note; a few months before the Mandate dissolved, Zionist Israeli forces, called the Haganah, annexed depopulated towns and villages in Mandatory Palestine in their Plan Dalet, the purpose of the plan being to acquire more territory for their future Jewish state.

The Arab nations, though it was predicated to be an easy victory, lost. They lacked strength, cooperation, and unity. Their forces were weak compared to Israel's, who had acquired a ton of artillery post-WWII. They'd also received a great amount of military and economic support from other powerful nations: Czechoslovakia, the US, and the USSR. The Arab nations had conflicting goals for the territory, which parts they wanted and how much, and were just generally unstable and distrusting of one another.

After the war, Israel secured a vast amount of territory, while the Gaza Strip was taken over by Egypt and the West Bank and the East of Jerusalem by Jordan. There was never an independent Palestinian Arab state created like it was described in the UN Partition Plan. In 1949, the Green Line was created (the red line in the map below). It was a ceasefire border determining Israel’s exact boundaries drawn by Israel and the Arab countries that neighbored it: Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria.



This war also led to the displacement of 700,000 Palestinian Arabs, which is known as the Nakba, or the Catastrophe in English. Many were either forcefully removed from the land by Zionist forces, or fled out of fear. Palestinian Arab homes and villages were destroyed, turning residents into refugees that escaped mainly to the areas of Israel (the Gaza Strip and the West Bank) that were controlled at the time by other Arab nations, or just to the Arab nations themselves. This same year, UN Resolution 194, known as the Right of Return, stated that any displaced Palestinian Arabs had the right to go back to their homes. This was rejected by Israel to keep a Jewish majority in their state. Something that is often overlooked is the fact that up to 900,000 Jews across the Arabic Middle East were similarly expelled during this war, even if they’d been living in those nations for thousands of years.

This war led to decades worth of conflict between Israel and its neighbors, a conflict that continues into today.

The 6 Day War

Between:

  • Egypt, Jordan, Syria
  • Israel
The 6 Day War was an attack on Israel by Syria, Egypt, and Jordan, with Israel emerging victorious as they (spoiler alert) eventually did in the Yom Kippur War. It happened in the June of 1967. Leading up to the war, tensions between Israel and its Arabic neighbours were rising. It was obvious that the Arab nations were mobilizing, with blockades being made and defense pacts formed. So, Israel attacked first, giving Egypt’s air forces a surprise airstrike, destroying 90% of them. They did the same to Jordan and Syria. Without air protection, a nation is incredibly vulnerable. Interestingly enough, regarding Jordan, Israel made an appeal asking them to stay out of the war, but they refused, and were consequently destroyed. The war ended after just six days with ceasefires negotiated by the UN. After their crushing defeat, the Arab nations created the Khartoum Declaration, stating a hardline no policy to recognition of, peace, and negotiations with Israel.

Israel nearly tripled in size, gaining the Sinai Peninsula and Gaza Strip from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria.

The Yom Kippur War

Between:

  • Egypt, Syria

  • Israel
The Yom Kippur War was an attack on Israel by Egypt and Syria. The two nations surprised Israel on the Jewish holiday of Yom Kippur, the war lasting from October 6th to the 25th of 1973. Egypt crossed the Suez Canal while Syria entered the Golan Heights. Their goal was to recapture these territories after the 6 Day War. The President of Egypt, Anwar Sadat, also believed that this invasion might force Israel to provide them with favorable peace negotiations.

At the beginning, Israel seemed to be losing. The Soviet Union provided the Arab nations with great amounts of funding, weaponry, etc. With more up-to-date arms, and an actually effective surprise attack, the Arab nations had improved since their first attempt in 1967. However, once Israel caught up, the tides began to turn. Israel also received a lot of support from the US in Operation Nickel Grass. This Operation airlifted tens of thousands tons worth of tanks, ammunition, etc. to Israel. US President Richard Nixon stepped in to not only save Israel from potential defeat, but also as a method of containment against the Soviet Union during the Cold War.

Israeli forces were able to successfully push back the Arab forces. Even though it was during a holy holiday, Israel mobilized so rapidly that they were able to reverse whatever early successes Egypt and Syria had. However, the fact that they’d even begun the war losing was enough to shake Israel into upping their game. Specifically, improving their intelligence departments to prevent being surprised in that manner ever again.

The war also led to the 1979 Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty, which ended the war between the two nations that had been ongoing for around 31 years. The Treaty was preceded by the Camp David Accords, which were political negotiations made in 1978 that addressed both the future of Israel and Egypt: rebuilding connections, the returning of the Sinai Peninsula to Egypt, and even discussion of Palestinian territories; the West Bank and Gaza. The Treaty also allowed for Egypt to begin receiving aid from the US. However, this Treaty upset the people of Egypt so immensely that Sadat was assassinated in 1981 over it.

Another impact of the war was the oil embargo on the US and other Western nations imposed by the Arab nations over their support of Israel. It caused a huge rise in gas prices, monetary inflation, and energy shortages worldwide. The embargo ended in 1974 when the US Secretary of State Henry Kissinger helped ease tensions between the Arab nations and Israel, mainly through Israel’s withdrawal from the Sinai Peninsula and Golan Heights.
